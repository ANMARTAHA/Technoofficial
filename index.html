01Core Idea

Pharmaceutical solutions are made by 4 preparation methods and used in 6 categories (oral, ophthalmic, parenteral, nasal, otic, other). Each use has its own requirements (sterility, isotonicity, pH/buffer, viscosity, preservatives, packaging), plus practical rules for expressing strength and speeding dissolution.

02Learning Points

  • Four preparation methods; simple solution is the easiest and is preferred when a solution can be made by simple solution or by chemical reaction.
  • Chemical reaction is chosen for 3 reasons: solute won't dissolve otherwise, active constituent usable only as a solution, desired by-product.
  • Solutions for sensitive areas of the body (anticoagulants, irrigating solutions, physiologic solutions) are prepared by simple solution with sterilization.
  • Oral solutions give systemic effects via GI absorption, expected to be more rapid than suspensions or solid dosage forms.
  • Ophthalmic solutions must be sterile, isotonic, buffered to pH 7.4, viscous, and properly packaged; preservative rules differ for multi-dose vs single-dose/surgical use.
  • Parenteral preparations have 6 general requirements and are classified by route (IV, IM, SC, ID, IS, IA, others) with different volumes and absorption properties.
  • Nasal solutions: adrenergic decongestants, isotonic, pH 5.5–6.5, short-term use. Otic solutions: cerumen removal and treatment of ear infection/inflammation/pain.
  • Strength is expressed as percent (w/v, v/v, w/w) or ratio strength for very dilute preparations.
  • Heat speeds dissolution for most agents (endothermic) but can destroy drugs, lose volatiles, and reduces solubility of exothermic agents such as calcium salts.

03Key Definitions

  • Simple solution: the easiest and simplest method of preparation (e.g. dissolving Ca(OH)2 in water).
  • Lime water: Ca(OH)2 solution.
  • Irrigating solution: used to flush or bathe wounds or surgical tissues; must be sterilized because it is used on sensitive areas of the body.
  • Ringer's solution (NF): isotonic solution of 6.6% NaCl, 0.3% KCl, 0.33% CaCl2 in purified water; sometimes called trichloride solution (isotonic solution of trichloride).
  • Isotonic: "equal tone". Hypertonic ophthalmic solution → solvent leaves the cell → shrinkage of eye tissue. Hypotonic → swelling of the tissue. Both cause discomfort.
  • Buffering of ophthalmic solutions: adjusting solution pH as close as possible to lachrymal fluid pH 7.4.
  • Micro-filter: a physical membrane whose pore size prevents bacteria and foreign materials from passing into the ophthalmic solution (less efficient than autoclave).
  • Parenteral preparations: injectable routes, used alone or diluted.
  • Cerumen (ear wax): combination of secretions of the sweat and sebaceous glands of the external auditory canal; if allowed to dry it forms a sticky semisolid holding epithelial cells, fallen hair, dust and foreign bodies.
  • Retention enema: injecting a solution into the rectum and holding it for a specific period of time.
  • Extemporaneous compounding: preparing an oral solution from bulk ingredients.
  • Unsaturated solutions: most pharmaceutical solutions; solute amount is well below the capacity of the solvent volume.

05Important Concepts

Choosing the preparation method

  • Simple solution is preferred if a solution could be made by either simple solution or chemical reaction (the lecture asks "Why?"). Example: Ca(OH)2 by dissolving in water vs CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2 by shaking lime with excess water.
  • Chemical reaction reasons: (1) pure solute may not dissolve from solid state otherwise — aluminum subacetate; (2) active constituent not obtained/not readily usable except as solution — formalin, H2O2; (3) by-product desired — Mg citrate.
  • Extraction: epinephrine solution is prepared with the help of hydrochloric acid.
  • Physiologic solution (Ringer's): cannot be used as a parenteral solution because its solvent is purified water, not water for injection.

Oral solutions

  • Prepared for the specific effects of the medicinal agents; intended for systemic effect (GI absorption into systemic circulation), expected more rapid than from suspension or solid forms.
  • Knowledge of solubility and stability of the agents and solvents helps the pharmacist advise whether the solution may be mixed with juice, milk, or other beverage.
  • Oral rehydration solution (ORS): typical liter = 45 mEq Na+, 20 mEq K+, 35 mEq Cl−, 30 mEq citrate, 25 g dextrose; liquid or powder packets; add the exact specified water amount; do not mix with other electrolyte-containing liquids (milk, fruit juice).
  • Magnesium citrate oral solution: colorless to slightly yellow, clear, effervescent, sweet, acidulous, lemon flavor; also called "citrate" or "citrate of magnesia"; a saline cathartic. It is an excellent medium for mold growth, so mold spores must be killed for stability.

Ophthalmic solutions

  • Sterile solutions for local effect or systemic effect (interior parts of the eye): isotonic · buffered to pH 7.4 · viscous · properly packaged volume.
  • Sterility: all or most are autoclaved at 121 °C (250 °F) for 15 min; unstable ones use micro-filtration (less efficient than autoclave) or gases/rays.
  • Multi-dose solutions must contain preservative so accidentally introduced microorganisms show no growth. Surgical / traumatized-eye solutions need no preservative because (1) single-dose, (2) preservatives irritate open eye tissue.
  • Preservative properties: effective at used concentration · inert (no interaction with active ingredient or container) · does not adsorb to container wall · stable.
  • Isotonicity: must be isotonic with lachrymal fluid; eye tolerates tonicity of about 0.6–2% without marked discomfort; boric acid 1.9% gives the same osmotic pressure as 0.9% NaCl (NaCl itself need not be used).
  • Buffering benefits: reduce patient discomfort · control stability · control therapeutic activity.
  • USP buffers: boric acid buffer (pH slightly below 5; suitable for many soluble salts of cocaine, phenylephrine, pilocarpine, tetracaine) · phosphate buffer (pH 5.9–8.0; suitable for many drugs except pilocarpine and homatropine salts, which are unstable in it).
  • Viscosity: must be viscous enough to prolong contact with eye tissue and prevent drainage; 1% methyl cellulose gives 25 cps (also used as tear replacement); optimal range 15–25 cps.
  • Packaging: easy administration, stable constituents, sterility maintained. Glass: alkaline substance leaches out and raises pH. Plastic: container interacts with the preservative or allows separation of some medicinal agents.

Parenteral preparations

General requirements (6): safety · sterility · free from pyrogen · clarity · stability · isotonicity.

  • I.V. advantages: very rapid response (drug goes directly to blood); great intensity (no drug loss); less irritant; great stability with some drugs vs I.M.; usable for large-volume fluids. Disadvantages: restricted to solutions; may cause hemolysis (avoid by slow injection); drug cannot be recovered if accidentally injected.
  • I.M.: easier than I.V.; solutions, suspensions, emulsions; drug stays in muscle and diffuses slowly → slower onset, prolonged action; absorption depends on drug release and blood flow; salt form absorbs faster than free form.
  • S.C.: slow onset; simple to administer; absorption affected by concentration, solubility, blood flow.
  • I.D.: local distribution, slow absorption, used for vaccines and diagnostic tests.
  • I.A.: needs surgical operation; targets a drug into an organ for diagnostic test; anti-neoplastic drugs.

Nasal solutions

  • Contain adrenergic agents for decongestant activity on nasal mucosa (local or systemic effect); e.g. Phenylephrine HCl solution USP (0.125–10%), vasoconstrictor, isotonic.
  • Approximately equal to 0.9% NaCl, buffered to normal nasal fluid pH (5.5–6.5), stabilized and preserved as required.
  • Best used for short periods (no longer than 3–5 days); do not exceed recommended dose and frequency.

Otic solutions

  • Placed in the ear canal as drops or small amounts to remove excessive cerumen or treat ear infections, inflammation, or pain.
  • Excess cerumen may cause itching, pain, impaired hearing.
  • Surfactant example: triethanolamine polypeptide oleate condensate 10% in propylene glycol emulsifies and disperses cerumen → removed by water irrigation. Carbamide peroxide: releases oxygen on contact with cerumen, disrupting impacted wax for easy removal.
  • The lecture asks why the vehicle is anhydrous glycerin or propylene glycol ("Why???").

Dissolution, heat and temperature

  • Most agents are more soluble at elevated temperature, because an endothermic solute–solvent reaction uses the heat energy to enhance dissolution.
  • Pharmacists are reluctant to use heat and never exceed the minimum required temperature: many agents are destroyed at high temperature and rapid dissolution may be offset by drug deterioration.
  • Heat must be avoided with volatile solutes or volatile solvents (e.g. alcohol): loss to the atmosphere.
  • Calcium salts undergo exothermic dissolution (give off heat) → heat discourages solution formation.
  • Calcium hydroxide (Calcium Hydroxide Topical Solution USP): 140 mg/100 mL at 25 °C (≈77 °F) vs 170 mg/100 mL at 15 °C (≈59 °F). Preparation/storage temperature affects final concentration.

07Processes / Stages

Magnesium citrate oral solution — preparation sequence

  • 1. React official magnesium carbonate with an excess of citric acid
  • 2. Flavor and sweeten with lemon oil and syrup
  • 3. Filter with talc
  • 4. Carbonate by adding potassium or sodium bicarbonate

Mold control: heat liquid to boiling → bring to volume with boiled water → rinse final container with boiling water → final solution may be sterilized. Refrigerated, lemon-flavored, carbonated product is accepted despite large volume and otherwise bitter taste.

Diarrhea → hypovolemic shock

  • Small intestine secretes far more than normal fluid and electrolytes
  • Exceeds large intestine's reabsorption ability
  • Fluid loss mostly from extracellular fluid compartment
  • Progressive loss of blood volume → hypovolemic shock (dehydration, possibly death, particularly in infants)

Ophthalmic sterilization decision

Autoclave 121 °C (250 °F), 15 min → if drug unstable: micro-filter (less efficient) → other options: gases and rays.

Cerumen accumulation

Sweat + sebaceous gland secretions → dry into sticky semisolid trapping epithelial cells, hair, dust, foreign bodies → excess causes itching, pain, impaired hearing.

Chlobutanol degradation

Hydrolysis/decomposition (fast at autoclave temp, slow at room temp) → HCl produced → microbial susceptibility + pH change → affects drug stability/physiologic activity → use efficient buffer.

08Examples

Simple solution

  • Amaranth solution USP 1% — in purified water; coloring agent for clear liquid preparations.
  • Gentian violet solution USP 1% — hydroalcoholic solution; topical local anti-infective.
  • Iodine solution NF — local anti-infective for skin; surgical disinfectant.
  • Tolnaftate solution USP 1% — non-aqueous homogeneous vehicle of polyethylene glycol 400.
  • Clindamycin phosphate topical solution — acne vulgaris.

Simple solution with sterilization

  • Heparin solution USP — sterile; 75,000 units sodium heparin in sodium chloride injection.
  • Citrate, phosphate, dextrose solution — sterile; 3% citric acid, 26.3% sodium citrate, 2.22% sodium biphosphate, 25.5% dextrose.
  • 15% amino acetic acid in water for injection — irrigating solution for operations such as prostatectomy.
  • Ringer's solution (NF) — 6.6% NaCl, 0.3% KCl, 0.33% CaCl2 in purified water.

Chemical reaction / extraction

  • Aluminum subacetate — astringent in wet dressing of eczema.
  • Formaldehyde solution (formalin) — disinfectant.
  • Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) — topical anti-infective to clean wounds; softens ear wax in certain dilution.
  • Mg citrate — cathartic.
  • Epinephrine solution (adrenalin chloride USP) 1:1000 in purified water, prepared with HCl — vasoconstrictor to increase blood pressure, prevent hemorrhage, and prolong local anesthetic action in dentistry.

Use-based examples

  • Oral: Oral rehydration solutions · Magnesium citrate oral solution.
  • Parenteral: insulin by S.C. route (soluble vs protamine zinc).
  • Nasal: Phenylephrine HCl USP (0.125–10%); oxymetazoline.
  • Otic: mineral/vegetable oils, hydrogen peroxide, triethanolamine polypeptide oleate condensate 10% in propylene glycol, carbamide peroxide in glycerin/propylene glycol.
  • Ophthalmic: 1% methyl cellulose (viscosity 25 cps, tear replacement); boric acid 1.9% (isotonic equivalent to 0.9% NaCl).
  • Other: Halazone tablets (disinfectant for sterilizing drinking water, not to be swallowed); potassium permanganate tablet (topical anti-infective, distilled water only).
  • Temperature-sensitive: Calcium Hydroxide Topical Solution USP.

09Clinical / Practical Importance

  • ORS: effective for mild volume depletion (5–10% of body weight), OTC, inexpensive, reduces complications associated with parenteral electrolyte solutions.
  • Counseling: pharmacist uses solubility/stability knowledge to advise on mixing with juice/milk; ORS powders need exact water volume and no milk/juice.
  • Mg citrate: pleasant way of taking an otherwise bitter saline cathartic.
  • Eye: isotonicity and buffering reduce discomfort; viscosity prevents drainage; fixed dropper lowers contamination risk.
  • Parenteral: IV hemolysis avoided by slow injection; IV drug cannot be recovered once injected.
  • Nasal: limit use to 3–5 days; respect dose and frequency.
  • Otic: cerumen removal; excess wax causes itching, pain, impaired hearing.
  • Heat: use minimum required temperature; avoid with volatiles; temperature affects Ca(OH)2 concentration.
  • Safety notes: Halazone not for swallowing; KMnO4 solutions with distilled water only; Ringer's (purified water) not for parenteral use.
